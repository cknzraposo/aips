## Proposed title

**From One Number to Many Realities: A Sector-Calibrated Policy Sandbox for AI Policy in New Zealand**

### Alternative titles
1. **Beyond the 44%: Modelling AI Policy Tradeoffs in a Heterogeneous New Zealand Economy**
2. **Why Single-Average AI Policy Fails: A Tiered Whole-Economy Framework for New Zealand**
3. **A Transparent NZ AI Policy Sandbox: Comparing Aggregate and Targeted AI Policy Under Uncertainty**

---

## Abstract outline

### Problem
New Zealand's AI policy discourse relies on an incoherent baseline. Widely cited metrics mix trust, worker usage, experimentation, and deployment as though they describe a single adoption reality.

### Contribution
This paper develops a transparent, tiered whole-economy policy sandbox that models AI policy tradeoffs across sectors rather than relying on a single national average.

### Method
A sector-calibrated scenario framework covering the full economy through a three-tier structure: nine explanatory sectors, six simplified sectors, and four residual economy blocks.

### Scenarios
- aggregate policy
- targeted demand-side policy
- targeted supply-side policy

### Claim
In a heterogeneous economy like New Zealand's, sector structure changes the effectiveness of AI policy. Policies designed around a single average are likely to misallocate effort.

### Output
A policy-relevant, transparent framework for comparing AI policy structures under uncertainty without overclaiming predictive precision.

# 1. Introduction

## 1.1 Opening problem
- AI policy debate in NZ increasingly uses national-level language
- the baseline is unstable and often misquoted
- policy discussion behaves as though AI adoption is a single measurable national state
- this obscures structural differences across sectors

## 1.2 Why New Zealand is a useful case
- small advanced economy
- mixed sector structure
- uneven digital maturity
- strong public-interest and regulatory dimensions
- AI discussion developing faster than robust measurement

## 1.3 Paper argument
The core argument is that a sector-calibrated, whole-economy policy sandbox provides a better basis for NZ AI policy reasoning than a single-average national framing.

## 1.4 Contribution
This paper contributes:
1. a baseline correction
2. a sector-calibrated policy framework
3. a transparent comparison of aggregate and targeted policy scenarios

# 5. Scenario Design

## 5.1 Scenario A - Aggregate policy
Economy-wide allocation by GDP share or equivalent broad rule.

## 5.2 Scenario B - Targeted demand-side policy
Direct support to lagging or high-leverage sectors.

## 5.3 Scenario C - Targeted supply-side policy
Investment in enabling sectors and diffusion infrastructure.

## 5.4 Why these scenarios matter
The real policy question is not only whether targeting beats aggregate allocation. It is also whether direct adoption support beats enabling-system investment.

# 6. Outputs and Evaluation Logic

## 6.1 Outputs the model should generate
- adoption trajectories
- sector productivity or output ranges
- labour pressure indicators
- spillover effects
- scenario comparison tables
- uncertainty bands

## 6.2 What the model will not claim
- exact GDP forecasts
- exact employment counts by future year
- definitive ranking precision where evidence is weak

## 6.3 Evaluation questions
- which policy structure performs best under which assumptions?
- where do sector bottlenecks dominate?
- where do social or governance constraints alter the economic story?
- where does whole-economy allocation dilute impact?

# 7. Anticipated Findings / Propositions

This section can be written as propositions before results are final.

### Proposition 1
Single-average AI policy is structurally weak in a heterogeneous economy.

### Proposition 2
Targeted policy is likely to outperform aggregate allocation where bottlenecks are sector-specific.

### Proposition 3
Supply-side investment may outperform direct sector targeting in cases where diffusion capacity is the binding constraint.

### Proposition 4
Some sectors require policy designs that balance productivity goals against equity, governance, or public-interest constraints.

### Proposition 5
A whole-economy denominator changes the honesty and interpretation of policy comparisons.

---

# 8. Limitations

## 8.1 Data limitations
- inconsistent adoption metrics
- uneven sector evidence
- weak productivity calibration in some sectors
- limited NZ-specific labour-market evidence

## 8.2 Modelling limitations
- scenario model, not prediction engine
- simplification in Tier 2 and Tier 3 sectors
- architecture still chosen for interpretability over maximum complexity

## 8.3 Interpretation limits
The paper should emphasise that useful policy comparison does not require certainty, but it does require transparency about what is inferred.

---

# 9. Policy Implications

## 9.1 For national AI strategy
- avoid leaning on a single adoption number
- distinguish between aggregate rhetoric and sector reality

## 9.2 For investment design
- compare broad allocation against sector bottleneck logic
- test when capability, procurement, or regulatory reform matter more than subsidy

## 9.3 For public communication
- move from headline adoption talk to structured tradeoff discussion

---

# 10. Conclusion

Return to the main claim:
- NZ needs a better frame for AI policy
- sector heterogeneity is not a detail but the core design problem
- transparent scenario modelling is more useful than confident but incoherent national averages

End with the project's modest but serious ambition:

> not to predict the future perfectly, but to make AI policy tradeoffs more honest, legible, and comparable.
